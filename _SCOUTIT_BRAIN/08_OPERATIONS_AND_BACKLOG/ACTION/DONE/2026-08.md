## A-037 — The ScoutIt Record is readable at a glance beside the advisor's identity

**Behavior now guaranteed.** The broker dossier carries a compact encoded
summary of the ScoutIt Record top-right beside the advisor's name, so the three
platform figures can be read without parsing a list. It renders on the canonical

**Owner direction, 2026-08-27.** A graph beside the profile, small enough to sit
next to the identity and large enough to read, gold marks on black, numbers
legible, sufficient on its own for the statistical metrics.

**The design constraint that shaped it.** The three metrics carry *incompatible
units* — a count, a percentage and a duration. Drawing 11, 92 and 26 against one
axis would present them as comparable magnitudes when they share no scale; this
is the single most common charting error. Each row is therefore encoded by what
its number actually is:

| Metric | Encoding | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Completed transactions | unit ticks, one per transaction | A count has no natural maximum, so no scale is invented |
| Response rate | meter | A true ratio against a real limit (100%) — the only honest bar of the three |
| Median reply | figure only | No sourced maximum exists |

**Why the duration deliberately has no bar.** Its only defensible denominator is
the 24-hour eligibility window the metric is defined against, and a median of 26
minutes inside 1,440 renders as under 2% of a track — a sliver that reads as
"almost nothing" while meaning "fast". Any friendlier ceiling would be a
benchmark ScoutIt never published, and a number repeated in an interface
acquires authority it never earned (Rule 12).

**Duplication removed.** The detail panel's numeric list was deleted because the
chart now carries the figures; that panel keeps provenance and method. This is
safe only while any publishable metric guarantees the chart renders, so that
invariant is pinned by an exhaustive test rather than assumed — if it broke, a
figure would be shown nowhere at all.

## A-045 — The dispute route finally has a screen, and the hold was watched working

**Behaviour now guaranteed.** A party to a conversation can report a problem
from the conversation itself — open or closed — choose a ground in plain
language, add context, and afterwards see that the report exists and what state
it is in. A-041 built the filing route in a way that was authorisation-correct
and well tested, and **nothing called it**, so in practice nobody could file.
That is Rule 13, and closing A-041 without recording it would have repeated the
exact defect A-041 fixed.

**The control is deliberately outside the `!closed` block**, like A-043's
download. A dispute is filed *against a conversation that has already ended*; a
control that disappeared at close would be missing at precisely the moment it
is needed.

**Two things in this surface are logic, not markup, and are tested as logic.**

1. **The grounds.** `DISPUTE_REASON_LABELS` lives beside `DISPUTE_REASONS` in
   `chatRetention.js` rather than in the component, because a label map kept
   elsewhere drifts from the list the route validates and *both directions are
   silent*: a missing label renders a ground nobody can choose, an extra one
   renders a ground that 400s after the person has typed their explanation.
   Tests assert the mapping in both directions and that no stored key is ever
   shown to a human.

2. **The window sentence.** "A right that expires unannounced is not a remedy",
   so the sentence has to be true for *this* deal. `describeDisputeWindow()`
   returns one of four states — still open, closed with no recorded date,
   counting down, or elapsed — and **decides "elapsed" using the purge job's own
   predicate** (`closed_at <= retentionCutoffIso()`) rather than a second
   arithmetic that happens to agree today. A thread closed exactly seven days
   ago is *already* purge-eligible; telling that person they have a day left
   would be the unannounced expiry this task exists to prevent.

**Who is told what about a dispute, and why it is not symmetric.** A hold
changes both parties' conversation — it stops the thread being purged — so both
are entitled to know it is under review, or the counterparty watches a closed
conversation quietly refuse to disappear and is told nothing. But the ground and
the free text are *the reporter's account of the other person*; handing "I was
abused or threatened" plus two thousand characters to the person it accuses
turns a complaint into a retaliation risk. The GET therefore returns full detail
to the reporter and hold-status only to anyone else, and the payload is built by
**addition rather than by deleting keys** — a response assembled by omission
leaks the day someone adds a column.

## A-051 -- One cross-workspace attention signal on the dashboard home (2026-08-29)

## A-053 -- The public catalogue may now be cached (2026-08-29)

The owner asked why the site still feels slow on a fast connection, and
described what they wanted by analogy: a song that keeps part of itself on the
device so the next play starts instantly.

**Bandwidth was never the constraint, and saying so mattered.** Past roughly 10
Mbps more speed changes nothing here. What is felt is round trips (~60-80ms
Manila to Seoul, per request), cold functions (3.4-4.4s measured on a cold
`/api/cms`), and re-downloading data the browser already had.

Code was already cached correctly -- `/_next/static/immutable/*` ships
`public, max-age=31536000, immutable`. An earlier claim in this session that
the bundles were uncached was wrong; it came from probing a 404 and reading the
document's header, and was corrected before anything was changed.

**Data was the opposite.** `/api/cms` sent `Cache-Control: no-store, private`:
the strongest possible instruction, forbidding both the browser and the CDN
from keeping any copy. 72 KB of public catalogue, identical for every visitor,
refetched from Airtable through a Seoul function on every page view by every
person.

**But that header was a deliberate security control, not an oversight.** The
route resolves the caller's tier and returns *more* data to subscribers --
`deepIntel`, enhanced photos, and Vault URLs whose leak makes the hosted asset
reachable forever. Its own comment says a shared cache could hand a Cluster
subscriber's unlocked catalogue to the next anonymous visitor. Adding
`s-maxage` as first recommended would have caused exactly that. The
recommendation was withdrawn before implementation.

**The fix follows the pattern `premiumFields.js` already documents** for the
ISR property page: the shared payload carries no premium values, and entitled
users fetch them separately. `/api/cms?scope=public` now strips to the
anonymous tier for *every* caller and never calls `resolveServerTier` at all --
not as an optimisation but as the guarantee that no session-derived value
exists in that branch to be cached. It returns
`public, s-maxage=60, stale-while-revalidate=300`; a visitor gets the cached
copy at once while a fresh one loads behind them, so a published listing
appears within the owner's chosen minute without anyone waiting on Airtable.
The bare route is untouched: still tier-resolved, still `no-store, private`,
still `Vary: Authorization, Cookie`.

Six surfaces moved to the cached scope -- homepage, Discover, DiscoverSearch,
Intel, the property directory, and the OSINT ticker. Each was checked against
all seven gated field names first and reads none of them. The three that do
read premium fields (CommercialFlow, ResidentialFlow, UnitMasterPage) stay on
the tier-resolved response, so no paid entitlement changed.

**Verification.** Against a production build: `?scope=public` returns
`public, s-maxage=60, stale-while-revalidate=300` with no auth `Vary`, the bare
route returns `no-store, private` with it. Inspecting the public payload,
all seven gated fields are present as empty values of the same shape
(`deepIntel: {}`, blank URLs) and carry no data -- `emptyLike`, so a component
mapping over `floorPlans` cannot crash on an absence. Six contract tests pin
the header split, the constant tier, the 60s window, and which callers may use
which scope. Full gate: 3/3 surface locks, ESLint, the 507-file typography
audit, 2,149/2,149 unit tests across 209 files, 584 E2E cases collected. The
A-027 layout-stability and discovery suites pass 24/24, so homepage CLS and
long-task budgets are unaffected.

**Boundary and what was dropped.** No service worker; that is the heavier
follow-up and it can serve a stale app shell after a deploy, so it wants its
own pass. The branded entry/loading screen discussed in the same conversation
was **dropped by the owner** once caching was understood -- correctly: a
loading screen cannot make anything faster, only make an existing wait feel
shorter, and the honest fix was to remove the wait. The owner's own summary of
why the original symptom mattered: the site felt slow while holding 13
properties and zero deals.

## A-053 security review of the cached catalogue (2026-08-29)

Run on the owner's instruction to prioritise security over speed, against live
production rather than a local build.

### Audited and clean

- **No `Set-Cookie` on either branch.** A session cookie riding a `public`
  response is the classic cache-poisoning session leak. Confirmed absent.
- **`getCmsBundle()` never reads a request, header, cookie or session**, so the
  payload cannot depend on the caller.
- **`/api/preload/public`** — the other publicly cached route, reviewed here for
  the first time. Its `GET()` takes no request argument at all, so it is
  structurally incapable of reading a session, and it projects only
  slug/title/category/location.
- **Vercel returns `X-Vercel-Cache: BYPASS` for any request carrying
  `Authorization`.** Authenticated callers never receive a cached copy. An
  earlier reading of "the response varies by Authorization" was this and not an
  app defect: the only differing field was `source`, cached copy versus fresh
  origin.
- **Proven at the origin, CDN out of the picture:** the public response is
  byte-identical under `Authorization`, `Cookie`, `X-Mock-User-Id`, and hostile
  `X-Forwarded-Host` / `X-Original-URL`. That is the property that makes public
  caching safe — it cannot leak one user's data to another because it does not
  depend on any user.

### Fixed

1. **The whole scheme rested on one ternary staying correct.**
   `findPremiumLeak()` now re-checks the finished array for any gated field
   still carrying data, and a response that fails is served `no-store` with a
   loud `console.error` instead. It converts a future stripping mistake from a
   silent paid-content leak — copied into the CDN and served to every visitor
   for the cache window — into an uncacheable response and a log line.
2. **`radius`/`lng`/`lat` are the CDN cache key and were unvalidated**, letting
   anyone mint unlimited distinct keys, each a fresh origin request running
   Haversine over the whole catalogue. Radius is bounded to 0 < r <= 2000km and
   coordinates to real ranges. This also fixed a latent bug: `?radius=abc` gave
   `parseFloat` NaN, every distance comparison failed, and the route returned
   **zero properties labelled as a legitimate radius search** — an empty
   directory presented as a real result. An unreadable centre now falls back to
   the default rather than cancelling the filter, because answering a radius
   search with the entire catalogue is worse than answering it from slightly
   the wrong point.
3. **`source` disclosed the backing store and its health** to anyone with curl:
   `airtable`, `upstash_redis`, `supabase_osint`, and `empty_fallback_on_error`,
   which announces a degraded backend. It now says only `radius` or `catalog`;
   the diagnostic detail stays server-side.

### Correction: the real freshness window is not 60 seconds

Earlier notes in this file, and what the owner was told, said a published or
withdrawn listing appears within a minute. **That was only the layer this work
added.** The true stack is:

| Layer | Window | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| `cmsCache` Redis (`ex: 600`) | 10 min | pre-existing |
| `cmsCache` in-process memory | 60s | pre-existing |
| CDN + browser (this work) | 60s fresh + 300s stale | new |

So a withdrawn listing can stay publicly visible for roughly **16 minutes**
worst case, of which about 10 predate this change. The 60-second header is the
*smallest* layer, not the governing one.

**This gap was closed the same day — see A-054 below.**

### Verified live after deploy

Cache headers correct on both branches; zero `Set-Cookie` on the cacheable
response; **7 properties with 0 gated fields carrying data**; internal source
names absent from the public payload; invalid, negative, zero and oversized
radii all fall back to the unfiltered catalogue while a valid radius still
filters 7 to 2. The directory renders 7 listings with no console errors and,
on a repeat visit, served the catalogue entirely from browser cache with no
network request at all.

## A-054 -- A takedown now purges the public catalogue (2026-08-29)

Closes the gap A-053's security review found: a listing removed for a legal or
privacy reason could not be evicted on demand and simply aged out of three
stacked caches, staying publicly visible for roughly sixteen minutes.

**The primitive already existed and was not being used.**
`invalidateCmsBundle()` was written for the broker dossier route and is careful
in exactly the right way -- it awaits any in-flight rebuild before clearing, so
a fetch that started before the takedown cannot repopulate the cache
immediately after it is emptied. Nothing else called it. Every route that
changes what the public can see -- withdraw, retained removal, publish and edit
-- wrote to the database and left the cached bundle untouched.

All four now purge:

| Route | Was | Now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `dashboard/archive` (withdraw) | up to 10 min visible | purged at once |
| `dashboard/delete` (removal) | up to 10 min visible | purged at once |
| `dashboard/publish` | up to 10 min invisible | purged at once |
| `dashboard/update` | up to 10 min stale | purged at once |

**A misplaced purge was caught before it shipped.** The first pass put the
removal purge inside the route's *idempotent repair* branch rather than the
real removal path, so an actual takedown would not have purged anything. The
contract test that checks each route purges after its write, not before, is
what makes that class of mistake visible instead of silent.

**Failure is logged, never propagated.** The removal has already been written;
reporting it as failed because a cache purge could not complete would invite a
retry of a destructive operation.

**The CDN cannot be purged, so it was bounded instead.** Nothing can reach a
copy already held by a CDN or a visitor's browser -- those can only age out.
`stale-while-revalidate` therefore drops from 300s to 60s, which makes that
window the deliberate hard floor on continued visibility rather than an
incidental performance setting.

**End-to-end effect on a takedown:**

| Layer | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Redis bundle | 10 min | purged immediately |
| In-process memory | 60s | cleared immediately |
| CDN + browser | 60s + 300s stale | 60s + 60s stale |
| **Worst case** | **~16 min** | **~2 min** |

**Verification.** `invalidateCmsBundle` is now covered behaviourally, not just
by shape: it deletes the `cms_bundle` key, is safe to call repeatedly as a
retried takedown would, and resolves cleanly when Redis is not configured at
all. Five contract tests pin that every mutating route imports and calls the
purge, does so after its write, swallows purge failures, and that the removal
route covers both its idempotent and real paths. Full gate: 3/3 owner-approved
surface locks, ESLint, the 507-file typography audit, 2,178/2,178 unit tests
across 213 files, 584 E2E cases collected, a clean production build, and the
dashboard-roles and LR-01 lifecycle-safety suites passing 40/40.

### What was delivered

mediation deliberately maps to `under_review` -- still a hold -- so picking a
dispute up can never be the act that exposes the conversation.

`disputes.deal_dispute_id`, `disputes.source` (checked to `staff` / `party`),

## A-060 -- A staff pin correction now reaches the public map (2026-08-30)

### The defect, re-verified before it was touched, and it was wider than recorded

`setVerifiedCoordinates` wrote `properties.coordinates` and `details.geo` and
stopped. Confirmed in current code: no Airtable call anywhere in
`dashboard/coordinates`.

**A second half the ACTIVE entry did not have.** Mission Control's own
`publishPropertyToAirtable` never sent `Latitude` / `Longitude` at all --
`listingFields` had no coordinate fields. Only the main app's owner publish path
carried them. So a staff-published listing reached the public site with no
position, and `/api/cms` fell back to geocoding the location text. The Position
Queue could not move a pin on the public map by **any** route, before or after
publication. Same omission class as finding W3 in the Airtable compression plan.

Live data makes it real rather than theoretical: 10 published properties, 1 with
coordinates.

### What was delivered

- `src/lib/geoPoint.mjs` -- one parser for PostGIS `POINT(lng lat)`, longitude
  first. Extracted rather than inlined because reading that pair backwards is
  how a Manila listing lands in the Pacific, and both values look plausible in
  review. Pinned by value in a test.
- `airtable.js` -- `coordinateFields()` folded into `listingFields`, so a staff
  publish now carries the pin; and `syncCoordinatesToAirtable()`, a deliberately
  narrow PATCH of `Latitude` / `Longitude` only. Republishing to move two
  numbers would rewrite ~90 fields and re-assert `Approved_For_ScoutIt`, so the
  correction path cannot publish, unpublish, or overwrite an owner's later edit.
  A slug absent from the public CMS is refused, not silently reported as fixed.
- `src/lib/publicCatalogueCache.js` -- the public site serves from a Redis
  `cms_bundle` key in front of Airtable, so a correction that reached Airtable
  still would not be seen. Purged over Upstash's REST API with `fetch`, no new
  dependency, matching how `airtable.js` here already talks to a service the
  main app uses a client for. The main app's separate 60-second in-process copy
  is unreachable from another deployment and deliberately not chased; it expires
  on its own.
- `coordinates/actions.js` -- the sync runs **before** the Supabase write, and a
  failed sync fails the whole correction. Writing Supabase first would drop the
  row out of the queue while the public map stayed wrong, which is the same
  invisibility the item exists to end. The action also had **no audit entry at
  all**; it now logs both the success and the failure path, carrying whether the
  public site actually took it.
